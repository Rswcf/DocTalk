## Question (d): subscription vs one-off for episodic use

Built only from the rows above.

- **Weekly plans (the shortest recurring unit).**
  - Weekly subscribers almost all leave within a year: median first-year retention is 1–2% in RevenueCat 2026 (row 9) and 3.4% in RevenueCat 2025 (row 11).
  - RevenueCat 2025 itself reads weekly subscriptions as working like short-term trials (row 11), and RevenueCat 2026 reads weekly-plan LTV as depending entirely on early monetization (row 9).
  - In Adapty's data, weekly plans are nonetheless the largest revenue share: 55.5% in 2025, up from 43.3% in 2023 (row 13). At upper-mid prices they start trials for 9.8% of installs, against 1.8% for annual and 0.3% for monthly plans (row 13).
  - Apps whose best seller is weekly earn $0.32 per install by day 60, against $0.29 for monthly-led and $0.46 for yearly-led apps (row 8).
  - All of this is mobile, auto-renewing, store-billed data.
- **One-time purchases and packs.**
  - No source isolates credit packs. The closest measures are "consumables" and "lifetime" in RevenueCat, and "one-time purchases" in Adapty.
  - Share of RevenueCat apps: 23.2% add a lifetime purchase and 10.7% add consumables (row 8). Among AI apps the figures are 17.9% and 12.5% (row 7).
  - Apps whose best seller is a lifetime plan earn the least per install by day 60: $0.24 (row 8).
  - In Adapty's data, one-time purchases are 17% of Education revenue in 2025, up from 6% in 2023 (row 15), and about 26% of Lifestyle revenue, up from 5.9% (row 16).
  - Adapty's AI apps start trials at about half the market rate (5.31% vs 10.92%) but make 14% more direct purchases (row 16).
- **Passes.** The only measured pass-to-subscription figure found is from news. One in eight Washington Post day-pass or week-pass buyers subscribed within 180 days (row 28). The figure is publisher-reported, has no comparison arm and does not say how many visitors bought a pass.
- **Low-priced subscriptions, the nearest proxy for an episodic buyer.**
  - SaaS businesses averaging under $10 per account per month: top-quartile 12-month customer retention 63.1%, top-quartile NRR 65.1% (row 22).
  - AI-native products under $50 a month: 23% GRR and 32% NRR. The report's author reads these payers as people who see themselves as being on a trial, and notes daily usage paywalls in AI products (row 21).
  - Median NRR on annual plans is 10–20 points higher than on monthly plans (row 21).
- **When the job is done inside the free period.** In the one randomized study that covers it, users who completed many premium tasks during the longer trial converted less at trial end (demand saturation) but more later, after promotions (row 30).
- **Not found.** No row measures what an episodic user does when a one-off unit and a subscription are offered at the same wall of a software product. See Gaps.

## Trials that depend on trial-end e-mail

Built only from the rows above.

- **No fetched source isolates the effect of a trial-end reminder or of lifecycle e-mail on trial conversion.** See Gaps.
- **Card-on-file (opt-out) trials convert at expiry by automatic billing, with no contact needed:**
  - RevenueCat's hard-paywall conversions spike to 25.7% at days 4–7, which the report attributes to 7-day trial expirations (row 3).
  - Users who do not want to pay cancel early: 55.4% of 3-day-trial cancellations happen on day 0 (row 5).
  - In the randomized SaaS study, "immediate conversion" is defined as that automatic billing (row 30).
  - Card-required B2B trials report about 30% free-to-paid, more than five times trials without a card (row 19).
  - In row 19, e-mail appears as a trust device before billing (Canva's reminder 7 days before its 30-day trial ends). The author names forgotten cancellations as a refund and retention risk, but measures nothing.
- **No-card trials and freemium convert later, at a moment the user has to be present for:**
  - 23% of freemium conversions arrive 6+ weeks after download (row 3), and 19.2% of all conversions arrive at week 6 or later (row 4).
  - The B2B benchmarks measure conversion over a 6-month window (rows 17, 23).
  - In the randomized freemium study, 41.3% of all conversions over two years were "delayed" conversions after the trial ended. The authors associate them with later promotions (a feature launch, a 20% discount); the paper does not name the channel (row 30).
  - Adapty reports that 23.5% of Education trial starts happen 31+ days after install, and names re-engagement — push notifications and e-mail win-backs — as the lever for that cohort. That is the author's reading, not a measurement (row 15).
- **Trial length.**
  - Longer store trials convert a larger share of starters: 42.5% for 17–32 days vs 25.5% for ≤4 days (row 5).
  - A SaaS experiment found 7 days beat 14 and 30 days (row 31).
  - The freemium experiment found 7 days vs 3 days raised delayed conversion but not conversion at trial end (row 30).
- **Reverse trials.** Measured once, in a self-reported survey: 7% of products use one; GOOD 4–6%, GREAT 8–12%; small sample, not statistically significant (row 17). The randomized study in row 30 has a reverse-trial structure (a premium trial inside freemium), but it compares trial lengths, not reverse trial vs no trial.
